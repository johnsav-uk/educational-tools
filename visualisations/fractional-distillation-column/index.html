Inside a tray. The capped spouts are the way up — vapour rises through them and is pushed out sideways under the cap, so it has to bubble through the liquid already lying there. The gap at the wall is the way down — liquid runs across the plate, spills over the weir into the channel at the wall, and the product pipe is fed from it. That channel has a solid floor: it is a draw-off sump, so the liquid it catches is piped away rather than draining on down to the tray below. Two flows, two separate paths, which is why a tray needs both.

Each fraction is drawn in the colour of the real product — colourless refinery gases at the top, through pale straw and amber, to black bitumen at the base. Boiling ranges and carbon numbers are typical industrial values, shown to make the gradient concrete. AQA does not require students to recall specific temperatures or colour — only the order of the fractions and the trends in boiling point, viscosity and flammability.
