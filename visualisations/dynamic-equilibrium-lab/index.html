The industrial compromise

  • Lower temperature gives more ammonia at equilibrium, but the reaction is too slow.
  • Higher temperature is faster, but less ammonia is left at equilibrium.
  • Higher pressure favours ammonia (4 molecules of gas become 2) and is faster, but thick-walled plant and compressors are expensive and use a lot of energy. About 200 atm is used.
  • The iron catalyst gets to equilibrium sooner. It does not change how much ammonia there is at equilibrium.
  • About 450 °C is a compromise: a reasonable yield, made quickly. Unreacted gases are recycled and ammonia is removed as a liquid.

Equilibrium constants

Kc from the chamber

At a fixed temperature, Kc has a constant value for a particular reaction.

Reaction quotient, Q

KcQ

Q has the same form as Kc, but it uses the concentrations now, whether or not the mixture is at equilibrium.

Q < Kc: too little product for equilibrium, so there is a net forward reaction. Q rises towards Kc.

Q = Kc: the mixture is at equilibrium. The forward and reverse rates are equal.

Q > Kc: too much product, so there is a net reverse reaction. Q falls towards Kc.

Change a concentration and Q moves while Kc stays put. Change the temperature and Kc moves instead.

What changes the equilibrium constant?

ChangeEquilibrium positionKc or Kp
Concentrationshifts to oppose it✕ no change
Pressureshifts if the gas moles differ✕ no change
Catalystno shift; reached sooner✕ no change
Temperatureshifts towards the endothermic side when heated✓ changes

Kc = kf / kr. A concentration or pressure change moves the concentrations, and the system rearranges until the ratio is back to Kc. A catalyst multiplies kf and kr by the same factor. Only temperature changes kf and kr by different amounts, because the forward and reverse activation energies differ by ΔH.

Heating: Kc increases if the forward reaction is endothermic and decreases if it is exothermic.

The Kc log on the page is the evidence: every row at the same temperature has the same Kc.
