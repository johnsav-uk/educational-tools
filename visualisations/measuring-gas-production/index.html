Why measure a gas?

When a reaction gives off a gas, the amount of gas made tells you how far the reaction has gone. Taking a reading every 10 seconds lets you plot a graph and work out the rate of reaction:

mean rate = quantity of product formedtime taken

The three setups

Press Play to start the reaction, or drag the time slider to any moment. Tick Live graph to plot the results as they come in, and Tangent to see the rate at that instant.

Method 1
Gas syringe: volume of gas against time

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2

How it works

  • The gas travels along the delivery tube and pushes the plunger out.
  • Read the volume in cm³ from the scale on the barrel every 10 s.
  • Start the stopwatch the moment the bung goes in.

Strengths

  • Measures the volume of any gas directly, including soluble ones like CO2.
  • Accurate: readings to the nearest 1 cm³.

Limitations

  • Holds only about 100 cm³; a fast reaction can push the plunger out.
  • The plunger can stick, then jump.

Method 2
Mass loss: balance and cotton wool

CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2

How it works

  • The flask sits on a digital balance. Gas escapes, so the mass falls.
  • The cotton wool lets the gas out but stops acid spray escaping.
  • Mass lost = mass of gas produced.

Strengths

  • Simple, and the balance can be read very precisely (0.01 g).
  • Works well for a dense gas like CO2 (Mr = 44).

Limitations

  • Useless for hydrogen: H2 is so light the mass barely changes.
  • The gas goes into the room, so avoid it for toxic gases.

Method 3
Downward displacement of water

2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2 (MnO2 catalyst)

How it works

  • A measuring cylinder is filled with water and turned upside down in a trough.
  • Gas bubbles up into it and pushes the water down.
  • Read the volume at the water level. The scale is upside down.

Strengths

  • Cheap, simple apparatus; you can see the gas collect.
  • Good for gases that are insoluble in water: H2, O2.

Limitations

  • Soluble gases dissolve (CO2 a little, NH3 and HCl a lot), so the volume reads low.
  • Upside-down scale; less precise than a syringe.

Comparing the three methods

Each method measures the same thing, how much gas has been made, but each one suits some gases better than others.

Two questions to ask

1. Does the gas dissolve in water? If it does, collecting it over water loses some of it, so use a gas syringe.

2. Is the gas heavy enough to weigh? A mole of H2 weighs only 2 g, so the mass change is too small to measure accurately. A mole of CO2 weighs 44 g, which a balance picks up easily.

Whichever method you use, the shape of the graph is the same: steep at first, then levelling off when a reactant is used up.

1Gas syringe

Equipment

  • Conical flask with a one-hole bung and delivery tube
  • Gas syringe (100 cm³), clamped
  • Stopwatch
  • Measuring cylinder for the acid
  • Magnesium ribbon and dilute hydrochloric acid

Method

  1. Check the syringe plunger is pushed fully in and reads 0 cm³.
  2. Measure 50 cm³ of acid into the flask.
  3. Add the magnesium, put the bung in straight away and start the stopwatch.
  4. Record the volume on the syringe every 10 s until it stops changing.
  5. Repeat with a different concentration of acid, keeping everything else the same.

Sources of error and improvements

ProblemEffectImprovement
Gas escapes before the bung is inVolumes too lowPut the acid in a small tube inside the flask and tip it over after the bung is in
Plunger sticksVolume jumps instead of rising smoothlyCheck the syringe moves freely before starting
Reaction too fastSyringe fills before the endUse less magnesium or more dilute acid
Leaky bung or jointsVolumes too lowCheck the seals are airtight

2Mass loss

Equipment

  • Conical flask and cotton wool plug
  • Digital balance reading to 0.01 g
  • Stopwatch
  • Marble chips (calcium carbonate) and dilute hydrochloric acid

Method

  1. Put the acid in the flask and the chips beside it on the balance, with the cotton wool.
  2. Add the chips to the acid, plug the neck with cotton wool and start the stopwatch.
  3. Record the mass every 10 s until it stops falling.
  4. Work out the mass lost at each time: starting mass − reading.

Sources of error and improvements

ProblemEffectImprovement
Acid spray escapes from the flaskMass loss too bigUse a cotton wool plug
Draughts push on the panReadings driftUse a draught shield; close windows
Gas is very light (H2)Change too small to measureUse a gas syringe instead
Some CO2 stays dissolved in the acidMass loss slightly low at firstSwirl the flask the same way each time

3Downward displacement of water

Equipment

  • Conical flask, bung and delivery tube
  • Trough (or large bowl) of water
  • 100 cm³ measuring cylinder, clamped upside down
  • Stopwatch
  • Hydrogen peroxide solution and manganese(IV) oxide catalyst

Method

  1. Fill the measuring cylinder completely with water, cover the top and turn it upside down in the trough.
  2. Clamp it with the open end under the water and place the end of the delivery tube underneath.
  3. Add the catalyst, put the bung in and start the stopwatch.
  4. Record the volume of gas every 10 s. Read at the water level; the scale runs downwards.

Sources of error and improvements

ProblemEffectImprovement
Gas dissolves in the waterVolumes too lowUse a gas syringe for soluble gases such as CO2
Air left in the cylinder at the startVolumes too high, or start reading not 0Fill completely; record the starting level
Reading the upside-down scaleMisread volumesRead at eye level at the bottom of the meniscus
Water sucked back into the flaskExperiment ruinedRemove the delivery tube from the water before anything cools
Common mistake

“The gas displaces the water downwards” describes what happens in the cylinder. Don't confuse it with downward delivery, a different way of collecting dense gases in a gas jar.

4Calculating rate from results

Mean rate

The mean (average) rate over a time interval is the change in quantity divided by the time taken:

rate = ΔVolumeΔTime  (cm³/s)   or   rate = ΔMassΔTime  (g/s)

Worked example

A gas syringe reads 0 cm³ at the start and 36 cm³ after 30 s. Mean rate = 36 ÷ 30 = 1.2 cm³/s.

A flask on a balance falls from 125.40 g to 124.80 g in 60 s. Mass lost = 0.60 g, so mean rate = 0.60 ÷ 60 = 0.010 g/s.

Units always come from the quantity divided by the time: cm³/s, g/s, and at Higher Tier mol/s. Rate can also be given per minute if time was measured in minutes.

Reading the graph

  • The steeper the line, the faster the reaction.
  • The curve is steepest at the start, when the reactants are most concentrated.
  • It gets less steep as reactants are used up, and goes flat when the reaction stops.
  • The height of the flat part is the total amount of gas. It depends on the amount of the limiting reactant, not on the rate.

Rate at a particular time: tangents HT

  1. Find the point on the curve at the time you want.
  2. Place a ruler so it just touches the curve there, without cutting across it, and draw the tangent.
  3. Make a large right-angled triangle under the tangent.
  4. Gradient = change in ychange in x = rate at that time.
Worked example

From the tangent at 20 s: Δy = 68 − 12 = 56 cm³ and Δx = 40 − 0 = 40 s. Rate = 56 ÷ 40 = 1.4 cm³/s.

Common mistakes

Reading the gradient from a tiny triangle: draw it as big as the tangent allows.

Using the curve's own value at that time (for example 40 cm³ ÷ 20 s). That gives the mean rate from the start, not the rate at 20 s.

Worksheet answers (teacher copy)

A. Syringe: volume, cm³; any gas / accurate; max 100 cm³ / plunger sticks. Mass loss: mass, g; precise balance / simple; no good for light gases (H2) / gas released into room. Over water: volume, cm³; cheap / see gas collect; soluble gases dissolve / upside-down scale.

B. (a) Gas syringe or over water: hydrogen is insoluble but too light for mass loss. (b) Mass loss or gas syringe: CO2 is dense (Mr 44) but slightly soluble in water. (c) The syringe: some CO2 dissolves in the water, so less is collected over water.

C. (b) 33 ÷ 20 = 1.65 ≈ 1.7 cm³/s. (c) About 0.7 cm³/s (accept 0.5–0.9), from a large triangle on a correctly drawn tangent.

D. Gas made in the first 5 s is lost / not measured, so every volume is too low (a systematic error) and the early rate looks slower. Tip the reactants together after the bung is in, e.g. acid in a small tube inside the flask, or start the stopwatch as the reactants meet.
