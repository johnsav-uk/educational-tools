Step 1 · Raw material & ore

From bauxite to alumina

  • Aluminium is extracted from the ore bauxite.
  • Bauxite contains aluminium oxide mixed with impurities, mostly iron oxide, which makes it red-brown.
  • It is purified to give white, powdery aluminium oxide, Al2O3, called alumina.
  • Aluminium is more reactive than carbon, so carbon cannot reduce aluminium oxide. We have to use electrolysis, which is much more expensive.

Metals below carbon can be reduced by heating with carbon. Aluminium sits above carbon, so electrolysis is the only way.

Step 2 · The melting-point problem

Why cryolite is added

  • Aluminium oxide is an ionic compound, made of Al3+ and O2− ions in a giant lattice.
  • Strong electrostatic forces between the ions give it a very high melting point: over 2000 °C.
  • For electrolysis the ions must be free to move, so the aluminium oxide has to be molten.
  • Instead, it is dissolved in molten cryolite. The mixture melts at about 950 °C.
  • A lower temperature means less energy is needed, which reduces costs.

Bonus fact: the molten mixture also conducts electricity well. The AQA marks are for lower melting point → less energy → lower cost.

Step 3 · The electrolytic cell

Inside the cell

  • A steel case holds everything.
  • The case is lined with carbon (graphite). The lining is the negative electrode (cathode).
  • Large carbon (graphite) blocks dip into the top. They are the positive electrodes (anodes).
  • The electrolyte is molten aluminium oxide dissolved in cryolite, at about 950 °C.
  • A huge direct current flows through it.

Remember PANIC: Positive is Anode, Negative Is Cathode.

Step 4a · At the negative electrode

Aluminium is made at the cathode

  • Positive Al3+ ions are attracted to the negative electrode.
  • Each ion gains 3 electrons and becomes an aluminium atom.
Al3+ + 3e− → AlHT
  • Gaining electrons is reduction, so the aluminium ions are reduced.
  • Aluminium melts at 660 °C, so it forms as a liquid. It is denser than the electrolyte, so it sinks to the bottom of the cell.
  • The molten aluminium is siphoned (tapped) off regularly.

Switch the current on and watch the pool grow, then tap it off.

Step 4b · At the positive electrode

Oxygen is made at the anodes

  • Negative O2− ions are attracted to the positive electrodes.
  • Each ion loses 2 electrons. Two oxygen atoms join to form an oxygen molecule.
2O2− → O2 + 4e−HT
  • Losing electrons is oxidation, so the oxide ions are oxidised.
  • Check the balance: 2 oxide ions carry 4 negative charges, so 4 electrons are released.

In the model, every 3 oxide ions that are discharged release exactly enough electrons for 2 aluminium ions. The counters stay at 4 Al : 3 O2.

Step 4c · Anode replacement

Why the anodes burn away

  • The anodes are made of carbon.
  • At about 950 °C, the oxygen made at the anodes reacts with the carbon.
C + O2 → CO2
  • This produces carbon dioxide gas, so the anodes burn away.
  • The positive electrodes must be replaced regularly, which adds to the cost.

Leave the current on until the anodes wear out. Once they stop dipping into the electrolyte the circuit is broken. Then press Replace anodes.

Step 5 · Putting it together

The whole process

Overall, aluminium oxide is split into aluminium and oxygen:

2Al2O3 → 4Al + 3O2

Electrolyte

Al2O3 dissolved in molten cryolite at about 950 °C, not over 2000 °C.

Cathode (−)

Carbon lining. Al3+ gains electrons (reduced) to give molten Al, which is tapped off.

Anode (+)

Carbon blocks. O2− loses electrons (oxidised) to give O2, which then burns the carbon to CO2.

Why it is expensive

  • Huge amounts of electricity
  • Energy to melt the electrolyte
  • Anodes replaced regularly

Environment

  • CO2 released from the anodes
  • Electricity often from fossil fuels
  • Recycling uses about 5% of the energy

Revision notes: extracting aluminium

AQA specification. GCSE Chemistry 4.4.3.3, Combined Science: Trilogy 5.4.3.3. “Aluminium is manufactured by the electrolysis of a molten mixture of aluminium oxide and cryolite using carbon as the positive electrode (anode).” You should be able to explain why a mixture is used as the electrolyte and explain why the positive electrode must be continually replaced. Half-equations are HT Higher Tier only.

1Why electrolysis?

  • Metals less reactive than carbon can be extracted by reduction with carbon, which is cheap.
  • Metals more reactive than carbon, like aluminium, cannot be reduced by carbon. They are extracted by electrolysis.
  • Electrolysis is expensive. It needs large amounts of energy to melt the compound and to produce the electric current.

2The ore

  • An ore is a rock that contains enough of a metal or metal compound to make extraction economical.
  • The ore of aluminium is bauxite. It contains aluminium oxide and impurities, mainly iron oxide.
  • Bauxite is purified to give pure aluminium oxide, Al2O3, also called alumina.

3The electrolyte and the job of cryolite

  • Aluminium oxide is ionic. It contains Al3+ and O2− ions.
  • A solid ionic compound cannot be electrolysed because its ions are held in a lattice. The compound must be molten so the ions are free to move to the electrodes.
  • Aluminium oxide has a very high melting point, over 2000 °C, because of the strong electrostatic forces between its oppositely charged ions.
  • It is dissolved in molten cryolite. The mixture melts at about 950 °C.
  • This lowers the melting point, so less energy is needed, which reduces the cost.
Common mistake Cryolite is not a catalyst. It does not speed up the reaction. It also lowers the melting point of the electrolyte (the aluminium oxide), not the melting point of aluminium.
Examiner tip “Explain why a mixture is used” is usually worth 3 marks, and each link in the chain earns one: cryolite lowers the melting point → so less energy is needed → so it is cheaper. Don’t stop after the first link.

Examiner tip: balancing the oxygen half-equation Oxygen is a diatomic gas, so write O2, never O. That needs two oxide ions, which carry 2 × 2− = 4− in total, so four electrons are released. Check that the charge is the same on both sides: left −4, right 0 + 4(−1) = −4. ✓
You can also write it as 2O2− − 4e− → O2. AQA accepts both.
Common mistakes
  • Writing O instead of O2, or writing 2e− instead of 4e−.
  • Putting the electrons on the wrong side. At the cathode electrons are gained, so they go on the left. At the anode electrons are lost, so they go on the right.
  • Mixing up the electrodes. Positive ions (cations) go to the cathode; negative ions (anions) go to the anode.
  • Saying electrons flow through the electrolyte. It is the ions that move in the electrolyte. Electrons only flow in the wires and electrodes.

6Oxidation and reduction: OIL RIG

OIL RIG Oxidation Is Loss of electrons. Reduction Is Gain of electrons.
  • Al3+ ions gain electrons at the cathode, so they are reduced.
  • O2− ions lose electrons at the anode, so they are oxidised.
  • In terms of oxygen, aluminium oxide loses its oxygen, so it is reduced too.
Use PANIC to remember the electrodes: Positive is Anode, Negative Is Cathode.

7Why the positive electrodes must be replaced

  • Oxygen is produced at the positive electrode.
  • The positive electrode is made of carbon.
  • At the high temperature, the carbon reacts with the oxygen: C + O2 → CO2
  • Carbon dioxide is formed, so the anode burns away and must be replaced regularly.
Examiner tip: the 4-mark answer Give every step of the chain: oxygen is formed at the anode → the anode is made of carbon → the carbon reacts with the oxygen → to form carbon dioxide, so the anode wears away. Answers that only say “it burns away” get 1 mark at most.
Common mistake Saying the anodes “dissolve”, “melt” or “corrode in the electrolyte”. They react with the oxygen that is produced at them.

8Economic and environmental factors

FactorWhy it matters
ElectricityA very large current is needed, so the electricity is the biggest cost. Plants are often built near cheap hydroelectric power.
HeatEnergy is needed to melt the electrolyte and keep it at about 950 °C.
Anode replacementCarbon anodes have to be made and replaced regularly, which costs money and releases CO2.
Carbon dioxideCO2 from the anodes, and from any fossil fuels burned to make the electricity, is a greenhouse gas.
MiningBauxite quarries scar the landscape, and purifying bauxite leaves waste.
RecyclingRecycling aluminium uses only about 5% of the energy of extraction and saves the ore.

9Key words

Ore
A rock containing enough metal or metal compound to make extraction economical.
Electrolysis
Using electricity to break down an ionic compound that is molten or dissolved.
Electrolyte
The molten or dissolved ionic compound that is broken down.
Cathode
The negative electrode. Positive ions go here and are reduced.
Anode
The positive electrode. Negative ions go here and are oxidised.
Cryolite
An aluminium compound added to lower the melting point of the electrolyte.
Half-equation
An equation showing what happens at one electrode, including the electrons.
