The structure of the Earth

Four layers: the crust, the mantle, the outer core and the inner core. It gets hotter and denser towards the centre.

The crust

A thin, solid, rocky shell, broken into tectonic plates. Oceanic crust is thin, dense basalt; continental crust is thicker, lighter granite.

The mantle

About 2,900 km thick, mostly solid. Its upper part, the asthenosphere, is semi-molten, so it can flow very slowly.

The outer core

Liquid iron and nickel at 4,000–5,000°C. Its movement creates Earth's magnetic field.

The inner core

Solid iron and nickel at about 5,500°C. It stays solid because the pressure is so great.

Where the heat comes from

Heat left over from Earth's formation, plus radioactive decay of elements such as uranium in the core and mantle.

Convection currents: rising

Mantle rock near the core is heated, becomes less dense and rises slowly towards the crust.

Convection currents: spreading

Under the plates it spreads out sideways. Friction drags the plates above along, a few centimetres a year.

Convection currents: sinking

The rock cools, becomes denser and sinks back towards the core, where it is heated again. The loop repeats.

Slab pull and ridge push

Slab pull: a cold, dense plate sinking at a trench drags the rest behind it. Ridge push: new crust slides down and away from a raised ridge.

Common mistake

0million years ago

Alfred Wegener, 1912

A German meteorologist proposed that the continents were once joined in one supercontinent, Pangaea, and have drifted apart.

The jigsaw fit

The coasts of South America and Africa fit together like jigsaw pieces. They fit even better at the edge of the continental shelf.

Fossil evidence: Mesosaurus

A small freshwater reptile found only in South America and southern Africa. It could not have swum across a salty ocean.

Fossil evidence: Glossopteris

A seed fern found across South America, Africa, India, Antarctica and Australia. Its seeds were too heavy to blow across oceans.

Geological evidence

The Appalachians match the Scottish Highlands and Scandinavia in rock type, age and structure. Close the Atlantic and they join up.

Climatic evidence: glacial deposits

Rocks scratched by ice about 300 million years ago lie in places that are tropical today, such as India and Africa.

Climatic evidence: coal in Antarctica

Coal forms from swamp forests. Antarctica must once have been somewhere far warmer than the South Pole.

Why was he rejected?

He had no mechanism: no force strong enough to move continents. Most geologists dismissed the theory until the 1960s.

Why the theory was rejected

  • No mechanism. His suggested forces (Earth's spin and tides) were millions of times too weak. This was the main reason.
  • He said continents ploughed through the ocean floor, which geologists knew was too strong.
  • He was a meteorologist, an outsider to geology.
  • "Land bridges" that had since sunk were used to explain the matching fossils.

Wegener died in Greenland in 1930, about 30 years before sea-floor spreading supplied the missing mechanism.

The missing mechanism

After the Second World War, sonar mapping found a mountain ridge down the middle of the Atlantic. In 1962 Harry Hess explained it: sea-floor spreading.

Step 1: magma rises at the ridge

At a constructive plate boundary the plates move apart. Basalt magma rises from the mantle into the gap.

Step 2: iron minerals line up

While the lava is hot, iron-rich minerals such as magnetite act like tiny compass needles and point along Earth's magnetic field.

Step 3: the rock hardens

As the lava cools and sets, the minerals are locked in place. The rock keeps a permanent record of the field's direction.

Step 4: the field reverses

Every so often Earth's magnetic field flips: magnetic north becomes south. New rock then records reversed polarity.

Step 5: the crust moves apart

New rock is split and carried away on both sides of the ridge, and fresh magma fills the gap behind it.

Step 6: symmetrical stripes

Bands of normal and reversed rock run parallel to the ridge. Each side is a mirror image of the other. Only spreading explains that.

Dating the crust

Rock is youngest at the ridge and older, under thicker sediment, further away. No ocean floor is older than about 180 million years.

Who found it

  • 1912 Wegener proposes continental drift
  • 1929 Arthur Holmes suggests mantle convection
  • 1950s Marie Tharp and Bruce Heezen map the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and its rift valley from sonar
  • 1962 Harry Hess proposes sea-floor spreading
  • 1963 Vine and Matthews explain the magnetic stripes
  • 1968 The Glomar Challenger drills the sea floor: rock gets older away from the ridge
