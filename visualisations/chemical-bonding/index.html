2 · Ionic bonding

When a metal reacts with a non-metal, electrons are transferred from the metal atom to the non-metal atom. The metal atom loses electrons and becomes a positive ion; the non-metal atom gains electrons and becomes a negative ion. Both ions end up with the electronic structure of a noble gas.

Giant ionic lattice

  • The ions form a giant regular lattice: each ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions.
  • Strong electrostatic forces of attraction act in all directions.
  • High melting and boiling points: a lot of energy is needed to overcome the many strong forces. Bigger charges (Mg²⁺, O²⁻) mean stronger forces.
  • Conduct electricity when melted or dissolved: the ions are free to move and carry charge. Not as a solid: the ions are fixed in place.
  • There are no molecules in an ionic compound: never write “intermolecular forces”.

3 · Covalent bonding

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms, usually non-metals. It is strong because of the electrostatic attraction between the shared pair (negative) and the nuclei of both atoms (positive).

Simple molecular substances

  • Low melting and boiling points: only weak intermolecular forces act between the molecules, and little energy is needed to overcome them.
  • The covalent bonds do not break when the substance melts or boils: steam is still H₂O molecules.
  • Do not conduct electricity: the molecules have no overall charge, and there are no ions or delocalised electrons free to move.

4 · Metallic bonding

Metal atoms lose their outer shell electrons, which become delocalised: free to move through the whole structure. What is left is a giant regular lattice of positive metal ions, packed closely in layers.

The metallic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the delocalised electrons.

PropertyExplanation
High melting and boiling pointsStrong metallic bonds: a lot of energy is needed to overcome the attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons.
Good conductors of electricityThe delocalised electrons move through the structure and carry charge. The positive ions stay in place and only vibrate.
Good conductors of heatThe delocalised electrons move through the structure and transfer energy quickly; vibrating ions also pass energy on.
Malleable (bend and shape)The atoms are in layers, and the layers slide over each other. The bonding isn't broken: the delocalised electrons still hold the ions in their new positions.

Alloys are harder than pure metals

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements. The atoms of different sizes distort the layers, so it is more difficult for the layers to slide over each other. That makes the alloy harder. It is not because the bonds are stronger.
