Exam & revision panel

Collision theory: chemical reactions can only occur when reacting particles collide with each other and with sufficient energy.

Collision frequency

How often the reacting particles collide — the number of collisions per second.

Anything that puts particles closer together, or makes them move faster, increases it.

Activation energy (Eₐ)

The minimum amount of energy that particles must have in order to react.

Measured in kJ/mol. Shown as the height of the "hump" on a reaction profile.

Successful collision

A collision in which the colliding particles have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, so a reaction happens.

Most collisions are unsuccessful — the particles simply bounce apart.

Catalyst

A substance that speeds up the rate of a reaction without being used up in the reaction itself. It provides a different reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.

Does not change the products, the overall energy change (ΔH), or the final yield. Enzymes are biological catalysts.

Gradient (slope)

The steepness of the line. On an amount-of-product vs time graph, the gradient is the rate of reaction at that moment.

Steeper line = faster reaction. Horizontal line (gradient 0) = the reaction has stopped.

Rate of reaction

How quickly reactants are used up or products are formed.

mean rate = quantity of reactant used ÷ time taken, or quantity of product formed ÷ time taken. Units: g/s, cm³/s or mol/s.

Limiting reactant

The reactant that is completely used up first. It fixes the maximum amount of product — the height of the plateau on the graph.

Higher tier only: to find the rate at a particular time, draw a tangent to the curve at that time and calculate its gradient (change in y ÷ change in x).
Every answer below is built from the two ideas the mark scheme rewards: collision frequency and proportion of collisions with energy ≥ Eₐ. Only temperature and a catalyst change the second one.

Increasing temperature 2 marks

Increasing the temperature transfers more energy to the particles, so they move faster. This means collisions are more frequent, and also a higher proportion of the particles have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy, so a higher proportion of collisions are successful. Both effects increase the rate of reaction.

Increasing concentration 2 marks

Increasing the concentration means there are more particles of reactant in the same volume of solution. The particles are closer together, so collisions between reacting particles are more frequent and the rate of reaction increases. The energy of each collision is not changed.

Increasing pressure (gases) 2 marks

Increasing the pressure squeezes the same number of gas particles into a smaller volume. The particles are closer together, so collisions are more frequent and the rate of reaction increases.

Increasing surface area 2 marks

Breaking the solid into smaller pieces increases the surface area to volume ratio. More particles of the solid are exposed to the particles in the solution, so collisions are more frequent and the rate of reaction increases.

Adding a catalyst 3 marks

A catalyst provides a different reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. This means a higher proportion of collisions have energy ≥ Eₐ, so more collisions are successful and the rate increases. The catalyst is not used up in the reaction and it does not change the products or the overall energy change.

Why the graph levels off 2 marks

As the reaction proceeds the reactants are used up, so their concentration falls, collisions become less frequent and the gradient becomes shallower. The line becomes horizontal when one reactant has been completely used up and the reaction has stopped.

Why two curves reach the same height 2 marks

Both experiments used the same amount (moles) of the limiting reactant, so the same amount of product is made. Changing the temperature, concentration, surface area or adding a catalyst changes only the rate, not the final yield.

Calculating rate from a graph Higher

Mean rate = change in volume ÷ change in time, read from the graph, e.g. 60 cm³ ÷ 40 s = 1.5 cm³/s. For the rate at a given time, draw a tangent to the curve at that time and calculate the gradient of the tangent, using as large a triangle as possible.

1 · "More collisions" is not enough

✗ "There are more collisions."
✓ "Collisions are more frequent" (or "there are more collisions per second"). The word frequent — or per second — is what earns the mark.

2 · Temperature needs BOTH points

A 2-mark temperature question needs more frequent collisions and a higher proportion of collisions with energy ≥ Eₐ. Writing only "particles move faster" scores 0–1.

3 · Only temperature changes particle speed

✗ "Higher concentration makes the particles move faster."
Concentration, pressure and surface area change how often particles meet, not how fast they move or how much energy they have.

4 · Rate is not the same as yield

A steeper curve finishes sooner, but the height of the plateau depends only on the amount of limiting reactant. Don't write that a catalyst or a higher temperature "makes more product".

5 · What a catalyst actually does

✗ "The catalyst lowers the activation energy of the reaction."
✓ "The catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy." It also does not change ΔH, and it is not used up (avoid "not involved" — it is involved, then regenerated).

6 · Reading the gradient

Always take the units from the axes: cm³ ÷ s gives cm³/s. A tangent must be a straight line touching the curve at one point — and use a big triangle so the reading is accurate.

7 · Thiosulfate "cross" experiment

The measurement is a time, so rate ∝ 1 ÷ time. A shorter time means a faster rate. Plotting time (not 1/time) against concentration gives a curve going the "wrong" way — say which you have plotted.

8 · Why the reaction stops

✗ "It runs out of energy."
✓ "One of the reactants has been completely used up."

9 · Describe, then explain

"Describe" = say what the graph shows (steep at first, gets shallower, becomes horizontal at 80 cm³ after 50 s — quote data!). "Explain" = give the collision-theory reason.

Required practical: investigate how changing the concentration of a solution affects the rate of a reaction, by measuring the volume of a gas produced and by observing a colour change / precipitate.

Method A — measuring the volume of gas

  • Magnesium ribbon + dilute hydrochloric acid in a conical flask, sealed with a bung and delivery tube to a gas syringe.
  • Record the volume of hydrogen every 10 s for 2–3 minutes; start the stopwatch as the bung goes in.
  • Repeat with different concentrations of acid; plot volume against time for each on the same axes.
IndependentConcentration of hydrochloric acid
DependentVolume of gas collected (cm³)
ControlMass, length and surface area of magnesium; volume of acid; temperature

Method B — turbidity ("disappearing cross")

  • Sodium thiosulfate solution + dilute hydrochloric acid in a flask placed over a printed black cross.
  • Time how long until the cross can no longer be seen through the cloudy sulfur precipitate.
  • Repeat with diluted thiosulfate, keeping the total volume the same by adding water.
  • rate ∝ 1 ÷ time — plot 1/t against concentration to get a straight line through the origin.

Safety

  • Eye protection throughout; hydrochloric acid is an irritant.
  • Method B produces sulfur dioxide — use a well-ventilated room; a hazard for asthma sufferers.
  • Hydrogen from Method A is flammable — no naked flames.

Improving the results

  • Repeat and calculate a mean; ignore anomalous results.
  • Method B is subjective — the same person should judge every run, or use a light sensor and data logger.
  • Use a water bath to keep the temperature constant.

How a catalyst works the high-jump analogy

Only athletes who clear the qualifying height get through to the final. Anyone who knocks the bar off goes back to the queue to try again. If the bar is high, few jumps succeed and filling the final takes a long time. Open a second pit with a lower bar and the athletes jump no higher than before, but far more of their jumps succeed. A catalyst does the same for the particles in a reaction: beside the arena, every jump is replayed as a particle climbing the hump on a reaction profile, to the same height.

How the analogy maps onto the reaction

  • Athletes waiting to jump→reactant particles
  • Each jump→a collision between reactant particles
  • The height an athlete reaches→the energy of the collision
  • The qualifying height (the bar)→the activation energy, Eₐ
  • Clearing the bar→a successful collision: the particles react
  • Knocking the bar off→an unsuccessful collision: the particles bounce apart and collide again later
  • A second pit with a lower bar→a catalyst: an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy
  • Athletes in the final→product made

What a catalyst changes, and what it doesn't

The athletes jump no higher than before. A catalyst gives the particles no extra energy. It lowers the energy they need, so a higher proportion of collisions have energy ≥ Eₐ, more collisions are successful each second and the rate increases.

Everyone reaches the final in the end, with or without the second pit. The catalyst changes how quickly, not how many: the same amount of product is made.

The second pit is still standing when the competition is over. A catalyst is not used up.

Say it the exam way

The first bar never moves. A catalyst doesn't change the original pathway: it provides a different pathway with a lower activation energy. Write that, not just "it lowers the activation energy".
